2 Always openno control at all

The stack is simply part of the throw. There is nothing to click because nothing is hidden — which the fold makes viable, since a stack is now nine rows rather than forty-five. Fewest moving parts by a distance. The cost is a span with several throws: three stacks at once is a long panel, and the reader did not ask for two of them.

3 One switch for the region

The Exceptions header gains a Stacks switch that opens every throw at once, and rows still toggle individually by click. Good when a reader wants to scan three stacks together — comparing where two throws came from is one click rather than three. Adds a control to the header, which is the one place in this panel that has stayed a label.

4 The escaped one opens itself

The throw that failed the span is opened on arrival; the caught ones stay shut behind a row click. The reasoning is that a reader who opens a failed span is there for exactly that throw, so the panel can answer before being asked. The risk is the opposite reading: an opened stack draws the eye, and on a span where the escaped throw is not the interesting one it puts the emphasis in the wrong place and adds a scroll.

If you want one answer

1. It removes the pill by making the thing the reader is already pointing at do the job, which is the whole ask. The chevron in the gutter is the one piece of chrome it adds, and it is the piece that says the row opens — without it the row is a click target nobody knows is there. The foot goes back to carrying only the verdict.

3 composes with it rather than competing: the region switch is 1 plus a way to open all three at once, and worth adding the day someone complains about clicking three rows. 2 is tempting for having no control at all, and is genuinely right for the common span with one throw — it only falls down at three.

4 is the one I would not take. Opening the escaped throw guesses what the reader came for, and an opened stack pulls the eye whether or not it deserves it.
