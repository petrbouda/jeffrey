✓Final — two rails + bar pins

Directions 1 and 2 combined, and extended to a second family. Every instant is drawn twice on purpose: once on a rail above the span rows, which nothing can fold or filter away, and once as a pin on the bar of the span it belongs to. Clicking a rail mark opens direction 1’s popover for the immediate read, and its footer is the bridge into the deep one: it selects the span underneath, which opens with the same entry highlighted in its detail panel. Two families, two rails: jeffrey.Notification (diamonds, coloured by severity) and jdk.JavaExceptionThrow (crosses, hollow when caught, red when they escaped) — split the way the toolbar already splits Blocking ops from I/O ops. jeffrey.Alert is gone: severity already says how serious a notification is. What combining buys

  • Both readings in one picture: the rail answers did anything happen during this request, and when; the pin and the badge answer which span it happened in. Neither direction could do both alone.
  • The popover is the fast read and the detail row is the slow one, and they are one gesture rather than two surfaces competing for the same click: the popover answers what did it say on the spot, its footer opens the span, and everything the span carries — events, flamegraph, the stack behind the throw — is one click further on.
  • Both rails share the popover, with the content each family needs: severity, type, category and source for a notification; the thrown class, the top frame and a way into the stack for an exception.
  • The rail is the safety net for everything the pins cannot draw: a folded subtree, a critical-path filter, a 2 ms span at the 0.4% floor width.
  • The pattern took a second family for free. Exceptions get their own rail, their own glyph and their own switch — the same split the toolbar already makes between Blocking ops and I/O ops, for the same reason: different suspicions, so hiding one must not take the other down with it.
  • The escaped throw is the real prize. Today trace_spans.error_type is a bare class name. Matched to its throw it gains a message, an instant and a stack — the span stops saying it failed and starts saying here is where.
  • Nothing new has to be ingested: jdk.JavaExceptionThrow is already parsed and already powers the Exceptions view. This is a correlation, not a new pipeline.

What it still costs

  • Every instant is now drawn twice. One source of truth and one shared hover state, or the rail and the pin will eventually disagree — and a picture that contradicts itself is worse than either half.
  • The popover costs a layer. It is a third surface inside a fullscreen modal, so Escape has three meanings again, and whatever it hangs over is hidden while it is open. Two placement rules keep the damage in the chrome rather than in the drawing: it is anchored on its own mark and pulled 70% of its width to the left, so a mark near the right edge cannot push it over the Duration column; and it opens toward the chrome — upward from the notification rail over the toolbar, downward from the exception rail, which has another rail directly above it. A mark in the last few percent of the track still needs the horizontal flip.
  • Exception volume is the real risk. A service that throws for control flow produces thousands per request; jdk.ExceptionStatistics exists precisely because throws are cheap to make and expensive to record. The rail needs a cap and a cluster mark before it meets one, and the caught/escaped split is what keeps the noise quiet meanwhile.
  • The panel edge names the region, not the row. Its colour is the worst entry the region holds, which points unambiguously while there is one CRITICAL and one escaped throw. A panel carrying four severe rows would need either the row-level accent back or a count in the header — the edge alone stops saying which.
  • Row badges are getting crowded: ✦ N and ✕ N beside the error badge, and five indicators on a folded row (look at the panel above). Past two families this needs a different answer.
  • The severity ramp still shares danger with the GC-pause band and warning with the critical-path marker. Shape carries the difference today — diamond, cross, band — and a fourth thing in that palette would break it.
  • A rail is per-thread-agnostic but a pin is not: a notification stamped with a spanId from another thread pins correctly, an exception attributed by thread window does not travel. That asymmetry is invisible on screen.

What it takes to build

  • One .wf-lane block per family and two overlay switches, all beside the three that exist; pins inside the existing .wf-track; two badges in .wf-name; two .sd-regions in TraceSpanInlineDetail.vue.
  • The regions’ left edge takes the colour of the worst entry each holds — the same worst-of walk the row badges already do, so it is one derived value rather than a hard-coded red. The severe row itself keeps only its tint.
  • The folded aggregates reuse the machinery already there for foldedCounts and errorDescendantCounts — same walk, two more accumulators.
  • Notifications need the ids; exceptions do not. A notification can be committed off-thread, so it must carry traceId/spanId from Tracer.current(). A throw is always on the throwing thread, so thread_hash plus innermost-containing-window is a sound attribution — the same interval arithmetic ThreadWindowEvents already does.
  • Both land in one derived table beside DERIVE_TRACE_SPANS, with a kind column. The escaped flag is a match between the throw’s thrownClass and trace_spans.error_type, computed once at derivation rather than in the UI.
  • A rail mark opens the popover locally; its footer emits the existing select event plus the entry id, so TraceSpansModal opens the span and the region scrolls to the row. The popover is the only genuinely new component in the design.
